\begin{abstract}
Convex hull pricing addresses nonconvex generation costs, but reduced lost-opportunity compensation does not by itself characterize profitable strategic offers. This paper develops a continuous-offer evaluation framework for network-constrained, multiperiod markets with complete reported lost-opportunity-cost uplift. An interval network-flow model provides the fixed pricing formulation. Common primal optima and complete endpoint duals construct a reusable selected-price map, while feasible schedule cost lines and global unit commitment bounds identify executable-schedule regions. Intersecting the two partitions reduces strategic-profit evaluation to finitely many endpoint and physical-plan candidates. The evaluation distinguishes an attained favorable-boundary maximum from a one-sided profit supremum under alternative adjacent-plan execution. A profit identity separates price effects from offer-exposure changes, and substitution costs predict physical switching thresholds. Modified IEEE systems demonstrate cumulative pricing speedups of 6.74, 20.54, and 2.00 for the longer measured workloads; a shorter workload does not amortize preprocessing. In the principal case, additional profit is 7,811.56 dollars while uplift decreases by 578.36 dollars, and a nearby interior offer earns 7,810.84 dollars. Temporal offer scope and transmission capacity shape this opportunity through price responses and feasible generation replacement.
\end{abstract}
\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Convex hull pricing, market power, parametric optimization, strategic offers, unit commitment, uplift payment.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{E}{lectricity} market prices coordinate generation decisions and determine the returns to participating producers. Startup costs, minimum operating times, and ramping constraints make day-ahead unit commitment (UC) nonconvex, so uniform energy prices may leave generators with lost opportunities relative to independent operation. Uplift payments compensate these opportunities under the applicable settlement rule. Convex hull pricing (CHP) incorporates commitment nonconvexities into price formation through the convexified system cost~\cite{gribik2007,hua2017}. As CHP is considered for market pricing and settlement, a central market-power question is how a producer can profit by changing its offer, and which operating conditions create that opportunity.

Existing studies establish that reducing lost-opportunity costs and encouraging truthful offers are distinct market-design objectives~\cite{sun2021strategic,wang2023incentive}. An offer change can alter nodal prices, the executable UC schedule, and the compensation calculated from reported costs. In a multiperiod network, these responses are coupled: a substitute may need to start earlier, incur additional online costs, or transfer power through a constrained line. Price sensitivity alone therefore does not determine the producer's additional profit. Repeated pointwise clearing and settlement can evaluate particular offers, but a continuous strategy range also requires locating the intervals and switching boundaries between them. The unresolved task is to evaluate that range with a consistent treatment of price selection, integer physical execution, and uplift, and then explain the economic source of the resulting gain.

This paper studies that task under network-constrained, multiperiod CHP and complete reported lost-opportunity-cost compensation. Building on an interval network-flow representation of unit operation, we follow a specified offer direction through price formation, physical clearing, and producer settlement. The resulting interval description supports reusable pricing and finite strategic-profit evaluation. It also connects profit changes to the cost of generation replacement and the transmission capacity available to that replacement. Modified IEEE systems illustrate how a producer can increase actual profit while receiving less uplift, how the timing of offer changes affects the available gain, and how transmission relaxation changes prices and feasible competition.

\subsection{Related Work}
Research on nonconvex market incentives distinguishes following an instructed dispatch from reporting actual costs. Wang \emph{et al.}~\cite{wang2023incentive} analyzed these incentive properties under alternative pricing and compensation schemes. Byers and Eldridge~\cite{byers2022auction} studied strategic self-commitment and self-scheduling through simulated bidder behavior in multiperiod markets. These contributions demonstrate why settlement design and bidding behavior must be assessed together. CHP-specific market-power research provides a complementary analytical foundation. Sun \emph{et al.}~\cite{sun2020market,sun2021strategic} characterized strategic gains and their relation to generator removal costs, while Wu and Sun~\cite{wu2021potential} examined fixed- and variable-cost offer manipulation. Our economic question builds on these results: for a given CHP and uplift rule, which continuous offers are profitable once complete physical execution and unit self-scheduling are evaluated consistently?

The distinction between influencing prices and earning additional profit is particularly relevant across time. Sun and Wu~\cite{sun2021temporal} derived offer-to-price sensitivities and a temporal vulnerability assessment for CHP. Such sensitivities describe how an offer perturbation propagates through the price vector. The producer's settlement additionally depends on the executable schedule, its best self-schedule at those prices, and the difference between reported and actual costs. We retain this temporal price-response perspective and connect it to a profit characterization over a finite offer range. The all-day and single-hour offer directions in the numerical study expose how the admissible timing of a strategy changes its profitable opportunities.

Computing these objects requires a reliable CHP representation. Hua and Baldick~\cite{hua2017} developed the convex-primal framework based on unit feasible-set hulls and convex cost envelopes. Extended UC formulations~\cite{pan2018,yu2019} and network-flow constructions~\cite{networkflow} attach dispatch and cost to legal operating intervals. Dantzig--Wolfe decomposition provides another exact computational route through unit-level subproblems~\cite{andrianesis2022dw}. These methods supply the convexified pricing problem used here. The network-flow foundation is retained in direct power coordinates, with the same operating and boundary conventions as physical clearing and self-scheduling. This consistency matters because changing a primal UC representation can change CHP~\cite{zhao2023dependence}.

Parametric linear programming supplies the value-function and duality properties used to reuse optimization information~\cite{bertsimas1997}. In the CHP literature, Jiang and Wu~\cite{jiang2026minkowski} developed a demand-to-price mapping using optimal supply sets and an approximated Minkowski sum. The present parameter enters reported cost rather than demand. Its economic output is also different: a legal price path must be combined with integer UC regions and reported-cost settlement to evaluate actual profit. Our method assembles these three objects into a continuous-offer evaluation procedure. Common primal and complete dual information establish the selected-price intervals; global UC bounds establish executable-schedule intervals; their intersection supports finite profit candidates.

\subsection{Our Contributions}
The contributions are organized around this progression from prices to executable schedules and economic outcomes.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Reusable parametric CHP pricing from a network-flow model.} We express affine offer changes as objective updates in the fixed network-flow pricing LP and construct a selected-price map using common primal optima and complete optimal endpoint duals. Adaptive value bounds identify where further optimization is needed, while shared endpoint duals retain a reproducible legal price path across the range. Measured cumulative costs include map construction and online queries.
\item \emph{Finite strategic-profit evaluation through physical UC regions.} We identify intervals with a common complete UC schedule using feasible cost lines and global mixed-integer lower bounds. Intersecting them with price segments yields regions on which actual profit is convex under complete reported uplift. This structure reduces evaluation of the continuous offer range to endpoint/physical-plan candidates. Boundary execution and one-sided branch limits are distinguished explicitly.
\item \emph{Settlement and substitution mechanisms of profitable market power.} We connect actual profit to reported self-scheduling profit and physical offer exposure, separating price effects from exposure and execution changes. A substitution-cost threshold predicts physical switching, while controlled cost and line-capacity comparisons explain how competing generation and network conditions shape the opportunity to earn additional profit. The analysis distinguishes a physical switching threshold from a profit-maximizing offer.
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:model} establishes the common market and network-flow formulation. Section~\ref{sec:method} develops the interval algorithms and finite-candidate result. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} interprets profit formation and substitution. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the computational and market evidence, followed by the conclusion.

\section{Market Model and Strategic Offers}\label{sec:model}
\subsection{Physical Scheduling and Offer Direction}
Let $\mathcal G$, $\mathcal B$, and $\mathcal L$ denote generators, buses, and lines, and let $t\in\mathcal T=\{1,\ldots,T\}$. Unit $i$ is connected to bus $n(i)$; $g$ denotes the strategic unit. Its schedule is $s_i=(\bp_i,\bu_i)\in\mathcal S_i$, where $u_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}$ and $p_{i,t}$ is active power. Time intervals are one hour. The fixed unit set $\mathcal S_i$ imposes minimum up/down times $M_i^\uparrow,M_i^\downarrow$, inherited operating conditions, and the following output constraints (written for a generic unit $g$):
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:physical}
\begin{align}
 \Pmin_g u_{g,t}&\le p_{g,t}\le\Pmax_g u_{g,t},\\
 p_{g,t}-p_{g,t-1}&\le R_g^\uparrow\quad (u_{g,t-1}=u_{g,t}=1),\\
 p_{g,t-1}-p_{g,t}&\le R_g^\downarrow\quad (u_{g,t-1}=u_{g,t}=1),\\
 p_{g,t}&\le S_g^\uparrow\quad (u_{g,t-1}=0,\ u_{g,t}=1),\\
 p_{g,t-1}&\le S_g^\downarrow\quad (u_{g,t-1}=1,\ u_{g,t}=0).
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The transition at $t=1$ uses the fixed preceding status $u_{g,0}$ and power $p_{g,0}$. Let $v_{g,t}^{\mathrm{SU}}$ and $v_{g,t}^{\mathrm{SD}}$ be binary startup and shutdown indicators. An explicit description of the commitment component is
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:commitment}
\begin{align}
 u_{g,t}-u_{g,t-1}&=v_{g,t}^{\mathrm{SU}}-v_{g,t}^{\mathrm{SD}},\\
 v_{g,t}^{\mathrm{SU}}+v_{g,t}^{\mathrm{SD}}&\le1,\\
 \sum_{\tau=\max\{1,t-M_g^\uparrow+1\}}^t v_{g,\tau}^{\mathrm{SU}}&\le u_{g,t},\\
 \sum_{\tau=\max\{1,t-M_g^\downarrow+1\}}^t v_{g,\tau}^{\mathrm{SD}}&\le1-u_{g,t}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The inherited residual up or down time fixes the required initial statuses. A terminal ON interval has no artificial shutdown or shutdown charge. These conventions are used unchanged in physical clearing, pricing, and self-scheduling; the chosen primal representation matters for CHP~\cite{zhao2023dependence}.

For one-hour intervals, actual operating cost is
\begin{align}
 C_g(s_g)={}&C_g^{\mathrm{SU}}n_g^{\mathrm{SU}}+C_g^{\mathrm{SD}}n_g^{\mathrm{SD}}
 +C_g^{\mathrm{NL}}\sum_tu_{g,t}\notag\\[-2pt]
 &+\sum_t\phi_g(p_{g,t}-\Pmin_g u_{g,t}),\label{eq:cost}
\end{align}
where $n_g^{\mathrm{SU}}=\sum_t v_{g,t}^{\mathrm{SU}}$, $n_g^{\mathrm{SD}}=\sum_t v_{g,t}^{\mathrm{SD}}$, and $\phi_g$ is convex piecewise linear with $\phi_g(0)=0$. The parameter $C_g^{\mathrm{NL}}$ covers operation at minimum output. For strategic unit $g$, define the quantity exposed to the offer increase by
\begin{equation}
 Q_g(s_g)=\sum_t w_t(p_{g,t}-\Pmin_g u_{g,t}),\qquad w_t\ge0.\label{eq:exposure}
\end{equation}
The reported cost is $\widehat C_g(s_g;\delta)=C_g(s_g)+\delta Q_g(s_g)$, with $\delta\in\mathcal D=[0,\bar\delta]$. Other units report actual cost: $\widehat C_i=C_i$ for $i\ne g$. The weights $w_t$ are fixed before evaluation. The principal strategy uses $w_t=1$ throughout the day; an hour-specific direction uses one nonzero weight. Thus $Q_g$ is measured in MWh and $\delta$ is an absolute incremental-cost adder in USD/MWh. Physical constraints and fixed costs remain the same along this direction.

Let $H_{\ell n}$ be the PTDF and $F_\ell$ the line limit. With demand $D_{n,t}$, the system-feasible set $\mathcal F^{\UC}$ imposes $s_i\in\mathcal S_i$ and
\begin{align}
 \sum_i p_{i,t}&=\sum_n D_{n,t},\label{eq:balance}\\
 f_{\ell,t}&=\sum_iH_{\ell,n(i)}p_{i,t}-\sum_nH_{\ell n}D_{n,t},\label{eq:network}\\
 -F_\ell&\le f_{\ell,t}\le F_\ell.\notag
\end{align}
Writing $C_{\mathrm{sys}}(s)=\sum_i C_i(s_i)$, the executable system schedule solves
\begin{equation}
 v^{\UC}(\delta)=\min_{s\in\mathcal F^{\UC}}
 \{C_{\mathrm{sys}}(s)+\delta Q_g(s_g)\}.\label{eq:physical-clearing}
\end{equation}
The considered market is physically feasible. The independent system operator (ISO) instructs an optimal schedule $s^{\UC}(\delta)$, with output $\bp^{\UC}(\delta)$. A schedule here includes the complete dispatch and commitment trajectories of all units; the cost line used below retains both.

\subsection{CHP Through a Direct-Power Extended Formulation}
CHP replaces each unit's nonconvex operating set by its joint power--commitment--cost hull, retaining the system network constraints. To make the dependence on the offer explicit, define the actual-cost epigraph
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal Y_i=\{(\bp_i,\bu_i,\eta_i):
 (\bp_i,\bu_i)\in\mathcal S_i,\ \eta_i\ge C_i(s_i)\}.\label{eq:unit-hull}
\end{equation}
Commitment is retained in this lifted description because offer exposure~\eqref{eq:exposure} depends on both output and status. The convexified pricing problem is
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:hull-clearing}
\begin{align}
 v^{\CHP}(\delta)=\min\quad &\sum_i\eta_i+\delta Q_g(\bp_g,\bu_g)\\
 \mathrm{s.t.}\quad &(\bp_i,\bu_i,\eta_i)\in\conv(\mathcal Y_i),\quad\forall i,\\
 &\eqref{eq:balance},\ \eqref{eq:network},\quad -F_\ell\le f_{\ell,t}\le F_\ell.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Linear exposure can be evaluated on the convexified coordinates. Equivalently, one can convexify each reported-cost epigraph at a fixed $\delta$. The lifted form above makes it clear why changing $\delta$ need not change the pricing feasible set.

An interval network-flow formulation represents these hulls explicitly~\cite{pan2018,yu2019,networkflow}. For each unit, an alternating time-expanded directed acyclic graph connects legal ON and OFF intervals. A source-to-sink path is one commitment trajectory. Let $\mathcal E_i^{\mathrm{on}}$ denote ON arcs and $B_i$ the signed node--arc incidence matrix. With one unit of supply at the source and one unit of demand at the sink, the arc weights satisfy
\begin{equation}
 B_i z_i=b_i^{\mathrm{flow}},\qquad z_i\ge0.\label{eq:flow}
\end{equation}
Minimum operating times and inherited conditions determine the allowed arcs. Fractional flow decomposes into commitment paths: an ON arc can be shared by several full trajectories, avoiding explicit enumeration of every complete trajectory.

For an ON arc $e=[a,b]$, introduce scaled power $p_{g,e,t}$ directly. Its perspective dispatch constraints include
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:interval}
\begin{align}
 \Pmin_g z_{g,e}&\le p_{g,e,t}\le\Pmax_g z_{g,e},&&a\le t\le b,\\
 p_{g,e,t}-p_{g,e,t-1}&\le R_g^\uparrow z_{g,e},&&a<t\le b,\\
 p_{g,e,t-1}-p_{g,e,t}&\le R_g^\downarrow z_{g,e},&&a<t\le b.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
A new startup gives $p_{g,e,a}\le S_g^\uparrow z_{g,e}$; a following shutdown gives $p_{g,e,b}\le S_g^\downarrow z_{g,e}$. For an inherited ON arc beginning at $t=1$, the initial ramp inequalities instead use $p_{g,0}z_{g,e}$. These are direct scaled powers, not changes in power. If $\phi_g(q)=\max_k\{a_{gk}q+b_{gk}\}$, interval cost variables satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \xi_{g,e,t}\ge a_{gk}(p_{g,e,t}-\Pmin_g z_{g,e})+b_{gk}z_{g,e},\quad\forall k.\label{eq:perspective}
\end{equation}
Let $\mathcal E_i$ denote all ON and OFF arcs, and let $\kappa_{i,e}$ collect the fixed costs assigned to arc $e$, including online duration and actual state-transition charges. Output, status, and cost use the same arc weights:
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:aggregation}
\begin{align}
 p_{i,t}&=\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_i^{\mathrm{on}}:t\in e}p_{i,e,t},\\
 u_{i,t}&=\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_i^{\mathrm{on}}:t\in e}z_{i,e},\\
 \eta_i&\ge\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_i}\kappa_{i,e}z_{i,e}
 +\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_i^{\mathrm{on}}}\sum_{t\in e}\xi_{i,e,t}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Inherited immediate-shutdown charges, when applicable, are assigned to the corresponding initial OFF arc. They are included with the fixed arc costs in the same aggregation. At zero arc weight, capacity forces its output to zero. At positive weight, division by that weight recovers an ordinary feasible ON-interval trajectory and its cost. Flow decomposition then mixes those trajectories under common power and cost weights. This supplies the joint hull for the stated interval-local model; its construction is the computational foundation inherited from the network-flow approach.

For fixed physical parameters and an affine offer direction, the pricing problem has the form
\begin{align}
 v^{\CHP}(\delta)&=\min_{x\in\mathcal P}(c^0+\delta d)^\top x,\label{eq:paramlp}\\
 \mathcal P&=\{x:Ax=b,\ Gx\le h,\ \underline x\le x\le\overline x\}.\notag
\end{align}
Here $x$ collects arc-flow, direct-power, and cost variables. The offer direction has the explicit action
\begin{equation}
 d^\top x=\sum_t w_t\!\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_g^{\mathrm{on}}:t\in e}
 (p_{g,e,t}-\Pmin_g z_{g,e}).\label{eq:lp-direction}
\end{equation}
Consequently, $\mathcal P$ and the network matrices are fixed, and each new offer updates only objective coefficients. A change in demand, physical line limits, unit data, or inherited operating conditions defines a different instance whose map must be rebuilt or revalidated. The convexified pricing output $\bp^{\CHP}$ supports prices and can be fractional in commitment; the executable output remains $\bp^{\UC}$ from~\eqref{eq:physical-clearing}. The next subsection defines the independent unit response needed to turn these prices into settlement.

Let $\lambda_t^E$ be the multiplier on system balance, with nonnegative multipliers $\mu_{\ell,t}^{+}$ and $\mu_{\ell,t}^{-}$ on the upper and lower line limits. Nodal CHP is
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_{n,t}^{\CHP}=\lambda_t^E+\lambda_{n,t}^C,\qquad
 \lambda_{n,t}^C=-\sum_\ell H_{\ell n}(\mu_{\ell,t}^+-\mu_{\ell,t}^-).\label{eq:lmp}
\end{equation}
The system energy component is the balance multiplier of the constrained problem. Both components depend on the PTDF reference; nodal prices and settlement are invariant under a consistent reference change.

\subsection{Self-Scheduling, Uplift, and Actual Profit}
Given the selected nodal price vector $\blambda_g=(\lambda_{n(g),t}^{\CHP})_{t\in\mathcal T}$, a generator's best reported self-scheduling profit is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\Pi_g^{\SSched}(\blambda_g,\delta)=\max_{s_g\in\mathcal S_g}
 \{\blambda_g^\top\bp_g-\widehat C_g(s_g;\delta)\}.\label{eq:ss}
\end{equation}
Its optimizer $s_g^{\SSched}$ satisfies unit operating constraints; the collection of unit best responses need not balance system demand or satisfy network limits. This is the third dispatch object, distinct from physical clearing and convexified pricing output.

Actual energy revenue, uplift payment, and actual profit are
\begin{align}
 R_g(\delta)&=\blambda_g^{\CHP}(\delta)^\top\bp_g^{\UC}(\delta),\label{eq:revenue}\\
 U_g(\delta)&=\widehat\Pi_g^{\SSched}-[R_g(\delta)-\widehat C_g(s_g^{\UC};\delta)],\label{eq:uplift}\\
 \Pi_g(\delta)&=R_g(\delta)+U_g(\delta)-C_g(s_g^{\UC}(\delta)).\label{eq:profit}
\end{align}
Throughout this paper, uplift means the complete reported lost-opportunity-cost payment in~\eqref{eq:uplift}. Since $s_g^{\UC}$ is feasible for~\eqref{eq:ss}, $U_g\ge0$. Reported cost determines compensation, whereas actual operating cost determines actual profit.

For fixed other offers, the additional-profit objective is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal M_g=\max_{\delta\in[0,\bar\delta]}\Delta\Pi_g(\delta),\qquad
 \Delta\Pi_g(\delta)=\Pi_g(\delta)-\Pi_g(0).\label{eq:marketpower}
\end{equation}
The maximum is evaluated for a specified price and physical execution rule. We construct a legal price selection by complete-dual interpolation and use a common optimal schedule on each open physical region. At a boundary, both adjacent schedules are evaluated. Taking the higher-profit adjacent schedule defines the upper boundary candidate used in the main comparison. This is a potential-profit evaluation rule, not a producer instruction to the ISO. Section~\ref{sec:method} distinguishes its attained maximum from the one-sided supremum when a different adjacent schedule is executed at the boundary.

\section{Parametric Pricing and Finite Offer Evaluation}\label{sec:method}
The computational task is to evaluate actual profit over $\mathcal D$, following both a legal CHP price selection and executable physical clearing. The two optimization problems share the same scalar offer but have different feasible sets: the pricing problem is an LP on the unit hulls, whereas physical clearing retains integer commitment. We first construct price intervals, then find common physical schedules, and finally combine them for settlement. The finite-candidate result is the endpoint of this construction.

\subsection{Complete Duals and Affine Selected Prices}
The value of~\eqref{eq:paramlp} is concave and piecewise affine in $\delta$, a standard property of objective-parametric linear programs~\cite{bertsimas1997}. Its price vector is a projection of an optimal dual solution and need not be unique. The object constructed here is one legal selected-price path, supported by the full primal--dual information.

Write a complete LP dual as $y=(\pi,\nu,\zeta^L,\zeta^U)$. Its feasible set $\mathcal F^D(\delta)$ is defined by
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:dual-feasibility}
\begin{align}
 A^\top\pi+G^\top\nu+\zeta^L+\zeta^U&=c^0+\delta d,\\
 \nu\le0,\qquad \zeta^L\ge0,\qquad \zeta^U&\le0,
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\pi$ is free. Multipliers for absent infinite bounds are fixed to zero. The dual objective is
\begin{equation}
 L(y)=b^\top\pi+h^\top\nu
 +\underline x^\top\zeta^L+\overline x^\top\zeta^U,\label{eq:dual-value}
\end{equation}
with the bound terms summed over finite bounds only. Since all right-hand sides are fixed, $L$ is independent of $\delta$. The positive line multipliers in~\eqref{eq:lmp} equal the negatives of the corresponding entries of $\nu$. Retaining bound multipliers as well as row multipliers makes stationarity and strong duality checkable for the actual network-flow LP.

\begin{proposition}[Affine selected prices]\label{prop:dual}
Suppose one feasible $\bar x$ is optimal at $a<b$, and complete dual optima $y^a\in\mathcal F^D(a)$ and $y^b\in\mathcal F^D(b)$ attain its endpoint costs. For $\delta=(1-\theta)a+\theta b$, $0\le\theta\le1$, the vector $y(\delta)=(1-\theta)y^a+\theta y^b$ is dual optimal. Its nodal-price projection is affine on $[a,b]$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Interpolating~\eqref{eq:dual-feasibility} gives stationarity for $c^0+\delta d$ and preserves every multiplier sign. Furthermore,
\begin{align*}
 L(y(\delta))&=(1-\theta)L(y^a)+\theta L(y^b)\\
 &=(c^0+\delta d)^\top\bar x.
\end{align*}
Primal and dual feasibility with equal objectives establish optimality. Applying the linear price projection in~\eqref{eq:lmp} gives the stated affine path.
\end{proof}

This result uses the common primal optimum rather than a particular simplex basis. Degeneracy may permit several optimal bases and several legal prices; the complete endpoint duals select the path used for economic evaluation. Adjacent accepted intervals share their endpoint dual. Their price interpolation is therefore continuous at that endpoint even if the common primal solution changes.

\subsection{Adaptive Construction of Price Intervals}
At a solved offer $\delta_i$, retain a feasible primal $x^i$, a complete optimal dual $y^i$, and the coefficients of its affine feasible-cost line
\begin{equation}
 \ell_i^P(\delta)=A_i^P+\delta B_i^P,\quad
 A_i^P=(c^0)^\top x^i,\quad B_i^P=d^\top x^i.\label{eq:primal-line}
\end{equation}
For a bracket $[a,b]$, endpoint lines and dual values give
\begin{align}
 U_{ab}^P(\delta)&=\min\{\ell_a^P(\delta),\ell_b^P(\delta)\},\label{eq:pricing-upper}\\
 L_{ab}^P(\delta)&=\frac{b-\delta}{b-a}L(y^a)
                +\frac{\delta-a}{b-a}L(y^b).\label{eq:pricing-lower}
\end{align}
Every feasible primal line upper-bounds $v^{\CHP}$, while the convex combination of endpoint duals is feasible at the interpolated objective and supplies a lower bound. Thus $L_{ab}^P\le v^{\CHP}\le U_{ab}^P$.

The gap $U_{ab}^P-L_{ab}^P$ identifies where another solve can improve the interval description. With two endpoint lines, an interior maximizer occurs at their crossing when that crossing lies strictly inside the bracket; otherwise the procedure uses its midpoint. Among unresolved brackets, the largest remaining gap is refined first. Each new solve updates the fixed LP's objective, obtains a new complete primal--dual pair, and splits the bracket.

A small value gap alone is not the price certificate. For the pool $\mathcal W^P$ of saved feasible primals, the procedure also tests the common-primal discrepancy
\begin{equation}
 \gamma_{ab}^P=
 \min_{x\in\mathcal W^P}
 \max_{q\in\{a,b\}}\big|(c^0+qd)^\top x-L(y^q)\big|.\label{eq:pricing-common}
\end{equation}
In exact arithmetic, $\gamma_{ab}^P=0$ supplies the common optimum of Proposition~\ref{prop:dual}. Numerically, the endpoint primal feasibility, dual signs, stationarity, complementarity, and duality gap are checked, followed by the specified objective-gap tolerance. An interval opens for price queries only after these checks pass. A partial map retains its unresolved brackets if the solve budget is reached.

The selected-price intervals and value intervals can then be compressed separately. A union of value intervals is merged when a single feasible primal remains optimal at its outer endpoints. A selected-price breakpoint can be removed when the nodal prices remain collinear under the original selection. These operations preserve different objects, explaining why price and value segment counts can differ.

\subsection{Constructing Common Physical UC Regions}
Physical clearing requires its own interval search. A feasible complete system schedule $s^j$ defines
\begin{equation}
 \ell_j^U(\delta)=A_j^U+\delta B_j^U,\quad
 A_j^U=C_{\mathrm{sys}}(s^j),\quad B_j^U=Q_g(s_g^j).\label{eq:uc-line}
\end{equation}
It is feasible for every $\delta$ because the offer changes cost only. Consequently, $v^{\UC}$ is the lower envelope of these schedule lines over all feasible schedules. It is concave even though the schedules contain integer commitments.

\begin{lemma}[Common physical schedule]\label{lem:uc}
If a system-feasible schedule is optimal at both $a$ and $b$, it is optimal throughout $[a,b]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Its affine cost line upper-bounds $v^{\UC}$. Concavity lower-bounds $v^{\UC}$ by the chord of its endpoint values. When the schedule is optimal at both endpoints, its line equals that chord.
\end{proof}

To find such schedules, solve the physical UC at the domain endpoints, retaining complete feasible trajectories and valid global mixed-integer lower bounds $\underline v^U(a)$ and $\underline v^U(b)$. For the current schedule pool $\mathcal W^U$, choose the best common witness using
\begin{equation}
 \gamma_{ab}^U=
 \min_{s^j\in\mathcal W^U}
 \max_{q\in\{a,b\}}\big|\ell_j^U(q)-\underline v^U(q)\big|.\label{eq:uc-common}
\end{equation}
A zero discrepancy establishes the premise of Lemma~\ref{lem:uc}. For a numerical tolerance $\varepsilon_U$, it measures how closely one complete feasible schedule meets the global endpoint bounds. If schedule $j$ passes, the lower chord of these bounds implies, for $\delta=(1-\theta)a+\theta b$,
\begin{align}
 0\le\ell_j^U(\delta)-v^{\UC}(\delta)
 &\le(1-\theta)[\ell_j^U(a)-\underline v^U(a)]\notag\\
 &\quad+\theta[\ell_j^U(b)-\underline v^U(b)]
 \le\varepsilon_U.\label{eq:uc-uniform-gap}
\end{align}
Thus the same complete feasible schedule has a uniformly bounded objective discrepancy across the bracket. This is a cost certificate; it does not turn a small objective gap into a dispatch or profit error bound.

If no common schedule passes, let $s^a,s^b$ be the schedules returned at the bracket endpoints. Their candidate crossing is
\begin{equation}
 q_{ab}=\frac{A_b^U-A_a^U}{B_a^U-B_b^U},\label{eq:uc-probe}
\end{equation}
when the denominator is nonzero. Solve the global UC there if $q_{ab}\in(a,b)$; use the midpoint otherwise. A new competing schedule may enter and become a witness on one of the subintervals. The crossing proposes a useful next offer, while global UC lower bounds determine whether either candidate belongs to the optimal envelope. A better third schedule can displace both incumbents and is retained when the new global solve identifies it.

The implementation refines the bracket with the largest common-schedule discrepancy, repeats the endpoint tests on its subdivisions, and merges adjacent accepted intervals only when one saved schedule covers their union. It therefore identifies executable-schedule regions adaptively rather than inferring them from a dense predetermined offer grid. A full-domain assessment proceeds only after the accepted regions cover $\mathcal D$ without gaps.

\subsection{Joint Regions and Finite Profit Candidates}
Intersect the selected-price partition and common physical partition. Each joint interval $I_j=[a_j,b_j]$ has an affine selected price $\blambda_g(\delta)=\alpha_g^j+\delta\beta_g^j$ and a common complete system schedule $s^{\UC,j}$. The interval profit $\Pi_g^j$ evaluates that physical schedule throughout the interval, including its endpoint extension.

\begin{proposition}[Finite profit evaluation]\label{prop:finite}
For a fixed unit feasible set and actual cost, complete reported uplift, an affine selected price on $I_j$, and a common physical schedule, $\Pi_g^j$ is convex and
\begin{equation}
 \max_{\delta\in[a_j,b_j]}\Pi_g^j(\delta)
 =\max\{\Pi_g^j(a_j),\Pi_g^j(b_j)\}.\label{eq:endpoint}
\end{equation}
A finite covering joint partition therefore reduces the maximum of these branch profits to its endpoint/physical-plan candidates.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Substituting~\eqref{eq:uplift} into~\eqref{eq:profit} gives
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_g^j(\delta)=\max_{s_g\in\mathcal S_g}
 \{a_g^j(s_g)+\delta b_g^j(s_g)\}
 +\delta Q_g(s_g^{\UC,j}),\label{eq:convex-profit}
\end{equation}
where $a_g^j(s_g)=(\alpha_g^j)^\top\bp_g-C_g(s_g)$ and $b_g^j(s_g)=(\beta_g^j)^\top\bp_g-Q_g(s_g)$. Every fixed self-schedule contributes an affine function of $\delta$. Their supremum is convex; the last term is affine. A convex function on a compact interval is bounded above by the larger endpoint value, which proves~\eqref{eq:endpoint}. Applying it to every covering interval gives the finite reduction.
\end{proof}

Let $\mathcal C=\{(a_j,s^{\UC,j}),(b_j,s^{\UC,j}):j\}$, retaining different physical plans at a shared offer. The candidate value is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal M_g^{\mathrm{cand}}
 =\max_{(\delta,s)\in\mathcal C}
   \{\Pi_g(\delta;s)-\Pi_g(0)\}.\label{eq:candidate-value}
\end{equation}
Unit self-scheduling is solved at these candidates to compute reported uplift and actual profit. One need not construct all optimal self-scheduling regions to find this value. Common self-schedules are needed only if a complete profit/uplift curve or a local channel decomposition is also desired.

The boundary rule determines whether the candidate value is attained or approached. The upper adjacent-plan rule takes the more profitable adjacent physical plan at a shared boundary, so $\mathcal M_g=\mathcal M_g^{\mathrm{cand}}$ under this rule. Write $\delta^{\Pi}$ for a maximizing offer. For another execution rule $\rho$ that follows the same common schedules in open intervals and chooses an adjacent plan at each boundary, continuity of each branch gives
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{\delta\in\mathcal D}
 [\Pi_g^\rho(\delta)-\Pi_g(0)]
 =\mathcal M_g^{\mathrm{cand}}.\label{eq:supremum}
\end{equation}
A maximizing boundary value need not be attained under that rule, but can be approached from its corresponding interval. Here branch continuity follows from the bounded unit output set and continuous cost in the self-scheduling problem. Every interior or adjacent-boundary execution is bounded by~\eqref{eq:candidate-value}; approaching a maximizing endpoint from its open interval gives the reverse inequality in~\eqref{eq:supremum}. At $\delta=0$, the common selected price and complete reported uplift make actual profit independent of physical tie selection. Equation~\eqref{eq:supremum} concerns these specified branch executions, not a choice among every system-optimal schedule at every offer. The numerical study reports both the upper boundary candidate and a directly settled nearby interior offer.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Parametric CHP and Profit Evaluation}\label{alg:workflow}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Fixed market data, target $g$, weights $w$, domain $\mathcal D$
\State Solve endpoint CHP LPs; retain full primal--dual pairs
\While{unresolved price brackets remain and budget permits}
 \State Test a common primal using~\eqref{eq:pricing-common}
 \State Accept by Proposition~\ref{prop:dual}, or solve at the value-gap probe
\EndWhile
\If{accepted price intervals do not cover $\mathcal D$}
 \State \Return partial mapping and unresolved brackets
\EndIf
\State Solve endpoint physical UCs; retain schedules and global bounds
\While{unresolved physical brackets remain and budget permits}
 \State Select the bracket with largest $\gamma_{ab}^U$
 \If{a common schedule passes~\eqref{eq:uc-common}}
  \State Accept the bracket with this complete schedule
 \Else
  \State Form cost-line crossing~\eqref{eq:uc-probe}; use midpoint if invalid
  \State Solve global UC at the probe and subdivide
 \EndIf
\EndWhile
\If{accepted physical intervals do not cover $\mathcal D$}
 \State \Return checkpoint and unresolved physical intervals
\EndIf
\State Merge only where a common witness covers the union
\State Intersect price and physical partitions to form $\mathcal C$
\For{each distinct endpoint/physical-plan pair in $\mathcal C$}
 \State Solve reported self-scheduling; calculate $R_g,U_g,C_g,\Pi_g$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mathcal M_g^{\mathrm{cand}}$, maximizing candidates, and side limits
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Implementation and Computational Scope}
The pricing model retains its sparse network-flow matrices, variable indices, and offer direction. Only the objective is updated at a new $\delta$; all primal and dual solution arrays are then extracted anew. Each physical UC solve supplies an incumbent complete schedule and a global bound. Interval certificates, physical plans, and selected endpoint duals are saved so interrupted construction can resume without changing the declared economic objects. Failure to complete coverage produces a partial result, as indicated in Algorithm~\ref{alg:workflow}.

For a completed map with $K$ selected-price segments, an online query uses $O(\log K)$ comparisons to locate the segment and affine arithmetic to return prices. Offline work depends on the discovered structure and LP/MIP solves; neither the full region count nor the integer-solve effort is claimed to be polynomial in the horizon. The measured pricing comparison therefore includes map construction, postprocessing, loading, and query costs. It is separate from the physical-region and self-scheduling computation used for profit evaluation.

The resulting method uses the network-flow CHP solver in a specific way: continuous offers are organized around shared optimality information, while executable UC regions supply the dispatch selection that settlement requires. The next section interprets the finite candidates economically, explaining how price effects accumulate within a region and how generation replacement changes profit at its boundaries.

\section{Profit Formation and Physical Substitution}\label{sec:mechanism}
Section~\ref{sec:method} identifies the prices and physical plans associated with profitable candidates. Their economic interpretation requires separating the value of the announced prices from the cost discrepancy created by the offer. This section derives that separation and connects physical switching to the expense of competing generation.
\subsection{Profit Identity and Local Channels}
The algebra used in Proposition~\ref{prop:finite} also gives an economic characterization applicable to any feasible physical execution:
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\Pi_g(\delta)=\widehat\Pi_g^{\SSched}(\blambda_g(\delta),\delta)
 +\delta Q_g(s_g^{\UC}(\delta)).}\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
The first term is the highest reported operating profit at the announced prices. The second restores the gap between reported and actual costs on the instructed schedule. At truthful bidding, actual profit equals the best self-scheduling profit. At positive adders, physical selection enters through the offer-exposed quantity.

For two optimal physical schedules $A$ and $B$ at the same offer and selected price,~\eqref{eq:identity} implies
\begin{equation}
 \Pi_g^A-\Pi_g^B=\delta\,[Q_g(s_g^A)-Q_g(s_g^B)].\label{eq:tie-profit}
\end{equation}
The common reported self-scheduling value cancels between the two executions. At truthful bidding this difference is zero; at a positive adder, the schedule retaining more offer-exposed generation earns more actual profit after complete uplift. The reported system-cost tie does not resolve this producer-level difference.

Within a differentiable joint region, an optimal self-schedule gives the envelope relation
\begin{align}
 \frac{d\Pi_g}{d\delta}
 &=\underbrace{(\bp_g^{\SSched})^\top\frac{d\blambda_g^{\CHP}}{d\delta}}_{\text{price effect}}
 +\underbrace{Q_g^{\UC}-Q_g^{\SSched}}_{\text{offer-exposure effect}}\label{eq:derivative}\\
 &=(\bp_g^{\SSched})^\top\frac{d\blambda^E}{d\delta}
 +(\bp_g^{\SSched})^\top\frac{d\blambda_g^C}{d\delta}
 +Q_g^{\UC}-Q_g^{\SSched}.\notag
\end{align}
At self-scheduling switches, the relation applies on differentiable pieces, which are sufficient for integration. If the selected price is continuous and physical execution changes at $b$, its actual-profit jump is
\begin{equation}
 J_g(b)=b[Q_g^{\UC,+}(b)-Q_g^{\UC,-}(b)].\label{eq:physical-jump}
\end{equation}
Thus, along a path from truthful bidding to offer $\delta_f$, the full change is
\begin{align}
 \Delta\Pi_g(\delta_f)={}&\int_0^{\delta_f}(\bp_g^{\SSched})^\top
               \frac{d\blambda_g^{\CHP}}{d\delta}\,d\delta\notag\\
 &+\int_0^{\delta_f}(Q_g^{\UC}-Q_g^{\SSched})\,d\delta
 +\sum_{b\in\mathcal K(\delta_f)}J_g(b).\label{eq:path-ledger}
\end{align}
Here $\mathcal K(\delta_f)$ includes the execution changes traversed by that path, including a terminal change only if the selected endpoint execution requires it. The upper candidate at a switch can therefore be evaluated by its incoming branch without adding the outgoing branch's drop. If an alternative price rule has a jump, its additional term is the change of $\widehat\Pi_g^{\SSched}$ between the two prices at the same offer, rather than a derivative approximation. The shared-endpoint price rule used here is continuous.

Equation~\eqref{eq:derivative} weights prices by the best self-schedule, whereas energy revenue in~\eqref{eq:revenue} uses actual cleared output. This explains why a positive price-related profit effect and the change in energy revenue have different magnitudes. It also connects temporal price vulnerability to a settlement-based profit measure without equating the two.

\subsection{Substitution Costs and Physical Switching Thresholds}
Let $\delta^{\UC}$ denote a threshold at which the optimal physical schedule changes, and retain $\delta^{\Pi}$ for a profit-maximizing offer under the specified execution rule. Consider competing complete schedules $A,B$ with actual system costs $C_A,C_B$ and target exposures $Q_A>Q_B$. Their reported cost lines meet at
\begin{equation}
 \delta^{\UC}=\frac{C_B-C_A}{Q_A-Q_B}.\label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
The numerator measures the additional actual system cost of the alternative; the denominator measures the offer-exposed generation it replaces. Below this crossing, the target's larger exposure is offset by the lower actual system cost of schedule A. Above it, the extra reported cost favors schedule B. Interpreting~\eqref{eq:threshold} as an actual clearing threshold requires that no third schedule dominate near the crossing, which is checked using the global UC bounds in Section~\ref{sec:method}.

The threshold $\delta^{\UC}$ follows from physical cost competition; $\delta^{\Pi}$ additionally depends on CHP and uplift. They need not coincide. When price-related gains accumulate on a physical region but replacement causes a downward profit jump at its boundary, the incoming side of $\delta^{\UC}$ becomes an economically relevant candidate for $\delta^{\Pi}$. The finite evaluation compares this candidate with every other joint-region endpoint, establishing whether it is the most profitable one.

If a specified cost perturbation changes schedule $j$ by $\epsilon\chi_j$, the candidate threshold becomes
\begin{equation}
 \delta^{\UC}(\epsilon)=\delta^{\UC}(0)+\epsilon\frac{\chi_B-\chi_A}{Q_A-Q_B}.\label{eq:threshold-shift}
\end{equation}
For an online-cost perturbation, $\chi_j$ is the substitute's online duration. Equal startup counts instead predict no threshold movement under a startup-cost perturbation. These directions distinguish additional online operation from the cost of initiating that operation.

\subsection{Transmission Prices and Feasible Competition}
Network constraints can affect both terms involved in strategic opportunity. They shape the optimal dual prices in~\eqref{eq:lmp} and restrict the alternative schedules available in~\eqref{eq:uc-line}. Energy/congestion accounting identifies price components under a fixed reference. A controlled change to a line limit additionally reveals changes in feasible competition. These two analyses are used together.

For settlement consistency, define
\begin{equation}
 \Delta^{\mathrm{TX}}=\sum_{\ell,t}
 [\mu_{\ell,t}^+(F_\ell-f_{\ell,t}^{\UC})
 +\mu_{\ell,t}^-(F_\ell+f_{\ell,t}^{\UC})].\label{eq:tx}
\end{equation}
To see the settlement relation, let $D^{\lambda}=\sum_{n,t}\lambda_{n,t}^{\CHP}D_{n,t}$. Strong duality and unit self-scheduling give
\begin{equation}
 v^{\CHP}=D^{\lambda}-\sum_{\ell,t}(\mu_{\ell,t}^++\mu_{\ell,t}^-)F_\ell-\sum_i\widehat\Pi_i^{\SSched}.\label{eq:dual-bridge}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:lmp} and physical balance also give $\sum_iR_i-D^{\lambda}=-\sum_{\ell,t}(\mu_{\ell,t}^+-\mu_{\ell,t}^-)f_{\ell,t}^{\UC}$. Substitution into~\eqref{eq:uplift} yields
\begin{equation}
 \sum_i U_i+\Delta^{\mathrm{TX}}=v^{\UC}(\delta)-v^{\CHP}(\delta).\label{eq:network-settlement}
\end{equation}
The values on the right use reported costs. The nonnegative transmission adjustment evaluates pricing multipliers against physical scheduled flows; it is distinct from a nodal congestion-price component or an individual generator's uplift. In particular, a binding physical line need not have a nonzero CHP multiplier.

\section{Numerical Results}\label{sec:results}
The studies use modified IEEE 30- and 118-bus systems with a 24-hour horizon, three incremental cost segments, and PTDF transmission constraints. The systems have six and 54 generators, respectively. All units are initially offline at zero output with minimum down times satisfied. Every optimization and settlement uses these same inherited conditions. The reference incremental slopes for the principal G1 case and the 118-bus G11 case are 41.982 and 36.33 USD/MWh. The 0--50\% normalized ranges therefore correspond to absolute adders $[0,20.991]$ and $[0,18.165]$ USD/MWh; percentages describe the offer parameter.

The archived timing workloads use COPT 8.0.6 and Python 3.13.5 on the same workstation. Persistent reoptimization includes setup, objective updates, optimization, and price extraction. Parametric totals include construction, postprocessing, loading, and lookup. Queries are fixed shuffled sequences. Physical UC and unit self-scheduling are evaluated separately for settlement; their cost is not included in pricing speedup ratios. Pricing intervals use complete-dual checks and a $10^{-6}$-dollar common-primal acceptance tolerance. Physical regions use global UC bounds with a $10^{-5}$-dollar cost tolerance. These are objective-gap acceptance tolerances, not bounds on dispatch or profit error. The archived checks and source fingerprints are distributed with the results. All reported profits use actual costs after full reported uplift.

G5's congestion component rises from $-58.5446$ to $-45.9592$, so its nodal CHP increases from 56.34 to 58.26 despite the lower energy component. G5's cleared hour-19 output increases from 41.42 to 73.01 MW. The line-30 pricing multiplier becomes zero, while another line restriction remains active.

The relaxed system can also use a substitute schedule with a line-30 flow magnitude of approximately 39.01 MW, exceeding the original 20.8 MW limit. The original A/B schedules remain feasible and retain their mutual cost intersection, but the new schedule dominates them by approximately $2{,}196.5$ near that intersection. Transmission expansion changes the set of economic competitors that constrain G1's offer.

Finally, use each network's own truthful baseline. G1 baseline profit is $37{,}289.31$ in the original network and $31{,}911.46$ after relaxation. At $\delta^{\UC}-0.001$, total profit falls from $45{,}100.15$ to $40{,}302.15$, while additional profit rises from $7{,}810.84$ to $8{,}390.69$. Immediately above the original threshold, the corresponding increments are $6{,}424.82$ and $8{,}392.45$. These fixed-offer comparisons show how the direction of a market-power change can differ from the direction of a total-profit change. They connect the transmission price mechanism with the physical alternatives that the market can execute.

\section{Conclusion}
Continuous strategic offers can be evaluated by jointly representing selected CHP prices and executable UC schedules over the offer range. The network-flow pricing model supplies the common LP structure, while physical cost competition identifies the schedules used for settlement. Their joint intervals support finite profit candidates and reusable price queries, with preprocessing costs accounted for in the measured workloads. Boundary maxima and approachable one-sided profits are distinct outputs of the same evaluation.

The market interpretation follows from combining prices with reported uplift and actual operating cost. In the principal case, additional producer profit rises while both individual and system uplift decline. The timing of admissible offer changes alters the gain, and substitute generation imposes a physical switching threshold that can be predicted from incremental replacement cost. Transmission capacity affects both nodal price differences and the feasible replacement schedules. These mechanisms provide a settlement-consistent explanation of how price influence becomes a profitable opportunity within the specified strategy set.
